The government wants to pay for **electricity grid upgrades at about 15 to 25 motorway service areas in England**, so they can host far more chargers for cars, vans, trucks, coaches and motorbikes. The plan, called the **Strategic Charging Infrastructure Scheme**, has an indicative budget of **£190 million**.

The scheme is proposed by the Office for Zero Emission Vehicles (OZEV). OZEV says it counts as a "Scheme of Particular Interest": individual subsidies are expected to top £1 million, and some firms could receive more than £25 million in total. So OZEV asked the Subsidy Advice Unit, which is part of the Competition and Markets Authority, to look at it. OZEV made that request on 18 September, the unit accepted it on 24 September, and it published its report on **4 November**. The report is non-binding advice: it doesn't approve or block the scheme, but it points out where OZEV's assessment needs more work.

## How the scheme would work

- **The government pays for the grid work.** OZEV would commission the upgrades at chosen sites, then reserve the extra power for service area operators or chargepoint companies to put in public chargers open to any electric vehicle.
- **Firms still pay something.** They'd pay a charge to use the reserved capacity, which OZEV would partly waive to make the sums work. They also pay for the chargers themselves and the rest of the on-site kit.
- **The reservation won't last forever.** OZEV currently plans to hold the capacity for three to five years. Anything not taken up by then goes to any energy user, without the subsidy.
- **Sites are picked mainly on need.** OZEV would focus on places where the gap between today's capacity and future needs is biggest, and where upgrading the grid isn't commercially viable. Whether the work can be delivered, the geographic spread and sharing support across operators also count. The aim is to meet expected charging demand at those sites until at least 2035, and in some cases until 2050.
- **The money** comes from the £400 million set aside in Spending Review 2025 for charging between 2026 and 2030.

## What the watchdog's advisers said

The report says OZEV has clearly described and evidenced what the scheme is for, and weighed its benefits against its downsides in detail. But it asks OZEV to do more work in several areas, including:

- giving more detail and evidence on how much **extra investment** the scheme brings, and how it will leave out sites where investment would have happened anyway;
- checking it has ways to make sure the money actually adds charging capacity;
- looking harder at the effect on competition, including on nearby charging sites away from motorway services.

Some key details still aren't settled: the exact size of the subsidy, the cap on connection costs it will cover, and how long the capacity will be reserved. The report says OZEV's assessment can't reach a firm conclusion on whether the scheme is proportionate, or whether it keeps harm to competition and investment as low as possible, until those are decided.

## What it means for you

If your vans do long motorway days, this is aimed at a common worry: finding a charger at the services. OZEV's stated aims include a visible and reliable long-distance charging network, and reducing range and charging anxiety for drivers and fleet operators.

Truck operators are included too. According to OZEV's assessment, as summarised in the report, electric HGV charging is at an early stage, with fewer than ten public locations.

**Nothing changes yet.** The scheme is still a proposal, and the report doesn't name any sites or give a start date. We'll report when OZEV confirms the final design.
