## The three ways to charge

| Where | Typical speed | Rough cost | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Home** (7kW wall charger) | A full charge overnight for most vans | Lowest, especially on an off-peak electric vehicle tariff | Sole traders and drivers who take vans home |
| **Depot or yard** (7–22kW, or rapid chargers) | Overnight, or faster with rapid units | Low, on a business tariff | Fleets returning to base |
| **Public rapid chargers** | Often 30–60 minutes for a big top-up | Highest, often several times home prices | Topping up mid-day; not for everyday charging |

## Charging at home

- You need **off-street parking**: a driveway or private space. Running cables across pavements is generally not allowed without council permission.
- A dedicated home charger is safer and faster than a 3-pin plug.
- If your employer provides the van, agree **how home charging is repaid** before you start, so drivers aren't out of pocket.

## Charging at a depot

- Count vehicles, daily miles and the hours vans are parked. That tells you how many chargers you need, and how powerful.
- **Check your site's electricity supply early.** Adding several chargers may need a bigger grid connection, which can take months or longer.
- **Smart charging** spreads charging through the night to avoid expensive peaks.

## Public charging

- Useful for topping up, but expensive for everyday use.
- Check the van's **rapid charging connector**: almost all new vans use CCS. Older Nissan e-NV200s use CHAdeMO, which is being phased out.
- High-roof vans and vans with racks may not fit under some charger canopies, so check before relying on a site.

## What affects range

- **Load:** a full van uses more energy.
- **Cold weather:** expect noticeably less range in winter, especially with the heater on.
- **Speed:** motorway driving uses far more than stop-start town work.
- **Battery age:** check battery health on used vans.
