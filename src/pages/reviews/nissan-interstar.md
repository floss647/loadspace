The Interstar-e is Nissan's electric large van, and it is a rebadged Renault. It shares its body and electric drivetrain with the [Renault Master E-Tech](/vans/renault-master/): it is built on the same platform, and physically it is a Renault with a new nose and different badges. So the question isn't whether it's a good van, it's whether the Nissan deal beats the Renault one.

Few UK road tests of the electric Interstar have been published yet. The ones we found were early drives, including one in left-hand-drive vans around Barcelona, so read this alongside our [Master E-Tech review](/reviews/renault-master/).

### What's different from the Master

The warranty, mainly. Nissan covers the van for five years or 100,000 miles, with five years of roadside assistance, where Renault's standard cover is three years. On both badges the battery is covered for eight years or 100,000 miles. Servicing is every two years or 24,000 miles.

The other differences are trims and the dealer. Nissan offers two trims, Acenta and Tekna, and Renault sells more vans in the UK than Nissan, so its dealers may be better set up for them. If your nearest Nissan van dealer is a long way off, that matters on a working van.

### Battery, range and charging

The electric van now comes only with the 87kWh battery, so the 40kWh option offered at launch has gone. Nissan's launch figure for the official range was 285 miles, but a January 2026 buying guide quotes 286, and we couldn't reach Nissan's own UK figures to settle it. Either way it's one of the longer ranges among large electric vans.

Rapid charging runs at up to 130kW, reported to add 157 miles of range in half an hour, and a 22kW AC charger takes it from 10% to 100% in less than four hours. More in our [charging guide](/guides/charging-an-electric-van/).

### Load area and payload

The panel van gives 10.8 cubic metres in the L2H2 and 13.0 in the L3H2, and the side door opening is 1,312mm wide. Reports differ on whether the high-roof L3H3 is offered as an electric van, so check with the dealer.

Payload figures vary between sources too. For a 3.5-tonne van they range from 1,125kg to 1,134kg. For the 4.0-tonne van, reported maximums run from 1,544kg through 1,625kg to 1,631kg. Ask for the plated figure for the exact van you're buying.

### On the road

Being mechanically identical to the Renault, by early accounts it is competent rather than exciting: happy carrying a load, with a quiet cab. Reports differ on performance, from slow to brisk. Expect a physical key to turn and a manual handbrake to release on early vans, which gets tedious on a multi-drop day. Front-wheel drive is the only layout for the UK.

### Trims and kit

Acenta is the one we'd pick. It has a 10.1-inch touchscreen with wireless Apple CarPlay and Android Auto, a 7.0-inch digital instrument display, rear parking sensors, manual air conditioning and heated, powered door mirrors. Tekna adds a heated windscreen, 360-degree parking sensors, a rear-view camera, automatic air conditioning and automatic lights and wipers. Both keep physical dials for the climate control.

### Alternatives

The obvious one is the [Renault Master E-Tech](/vans/renault-master/). Beyond that, look at the [Ford E-Transit](/vans/ford-transit/), [Mercedes-Benz eSprinter](/vans/mercedes-benz-sprinter/), [Citroën ë-Relay](/vans/citroen-relay/) and [Toyota Proace Max Electric](/vans/toyota-proace-max/).

### Price

We couldn't find a current UK list price for the electric van. Nissan hadn't announced prices for the electric van when the first reviews appeared in late 2024, and we found no confirmed figure since. Get written quotes from Nissan and Renault dealers for the same body and weight.
